A research diary · computer tests only · nothing approved for use

Before the implant, there is the bench.

A small computer program that tries to pull one voice out of a noisy crowd, for people who hear through cochlear implants. We test it only on a computer, against rules we wrote down before each try. It passes 13 of our 14 checks. This site is about the one it does not.

The fuzzier the line behind this page, the harder it is to hear one voice. Watch it clear up (or not) as you read.

Part 1 · The crowd

Music and talking in a crowd are still the hardest thing.

Graham was born with bilateral, sensorineural, severe-to-profound hearing loss. He wears bilateral Cochlear Nucleus 6 processors; that is what he uses, and nothing on this site has ever been used by him. His dad, who built this project, has normal hearing.

Crowds are where cochlear-implant users struggle most. A dance class on a lawn, a birthday table, a playground: many voices at once, and the one you need is no louder than the rest. That is where the −5 dB wall lives, and it is the only thing this research is about.

A cochlear implant delivers sound as electrical pulses on a short row of electrodes. In quiet, that works remarkably well. In a room full of other voices, the thing a listener needs most, one voice standing apart from many, is exactly what the signal makes hardest to recover.

Why this exists

This project exists because of Graham.

Part 2 · The program

A program that cleans sound as it arrives, without peeking ahead.

Why this matters

Most powerful noise-removal programs work like an editor: they listen to a whole sentence, then clean it up. That is great for recordings and useless for live hearing. If Graham's friend says something, he needs to hear it now, not after the sentence ends. Our model cleans the sound while it is still arriving, which is exactly what a hearing device would need.

Why it is fast

Your brain expects what you see and what you hear to line up. If sound arrives late, lips stop matching words and your own voice starts to sound strange. So a hearing device only gets a tiny slice of time to do its work. Our model needs about 6 thousandths of a second (5.88 ms) of sound before it can answer. That is roughly 20 times quicker than a blink.

How it works

  • It listens in tiny slices, each about 4 thousandths of a second long.
  • For every slice it asks one question: how much of this is the voice? Then it keeps the voice and turns the rest down.
  • It remembers about the last second of sound, which helps it recognise the voice it is following, but it never peeks ahead.
  • It is small on purpose: about 600,000 numbers. A single phone photo holds millions. Small enough that we can imagine it running on a phone or a hearing processor someday, though we have not timed it on one yet.

How we know it never cheats

After every training run we play it two recordings that start the same and then go different ways. If the model ever peeked ahead, its output for the shared beginning would change. It never does, down to the very last bit.

Inside the implant · what happens to sound

How a cochlear implant hears.

A hearing ear has thousands of tiny hair cells, each one listening for its own little sliver of pitch. A cochlear implant takes the place of all of them with one thin wire that has just 22 tiny electrodes along it, like trading thousands of piano keys for 22. Everything the microphone picks up, a voice, a crowd, a song, has to squeeze through those 22 spots.

  1. 01

    22 slices of pitch

    The implant splits every sound into 22 slices of pitch, like 22 piano keys running from low to high. Each slice has its own tiny contact on the wire inside the ear. Low sounds go deep into the ear's spiral and high sounds stay near the opening, which is the same way a hearing ear sorts sound.

  2. 02

    Only about 8 at once

    Many times every second, the implant checks which slices are loudest, and only those get to play. Implants like Graham's commonly play about 8 of the 22 at a time, and an audiologist sets the exact number for each person. Think of a piano where only 8 keys can sound at once: the tune comes through, but a lot of it goes missing.

  3. 03

    What gets lost

    Inside each slice, all the fine detail is blended into one thing: how loud that slice is. That detail is what lets you hear a voice rise and fall, the warmth or scratchiness that makes someone sound like themselves, and the notes in a song. The rhythm of talking and the shape of words still come through surprisingly well. In a crowd, other voices compete for the same 8 keys, and the loudest ones are not always the voice you want.

Listen to what the squeeze does to a voice.

Researchers have a way to give people with typical hearing a rough idea of an implant. They split a recording into the same 22 slices, keep only the 8 loudest at each moment, and rebuild each slice out of soft hissing noise, so the fine detail disappears much as it does in an implant. These clips were made that way. They are about as close as someone with typical hearing can get to what Graham might hear, and they are still only a rough sketch. Every person with an implant hears differently, and a brain that grows up with one learns to make sense of it in ways no recording can show.

Each clip is the same sentence at the same loudness, about 8 seconds long. Only one plays at a time.

How these clips were made

The voices come from LibriSpeech, a free collection of volunteers reading books aloud. Nothing from Graham or his family is used here. The reader in these clips, and the people in the crowd around her, were never used to teach the model, so it is hearing all of them for the first time. The crowd is ten snippets of other readers talking at once, set to be as loud as her or a little louder.

The squeeze into 8 of 22 slices is the same one this research uses to score the model, and the moving picture above is drawn from those same numbers. A real implant is more complicated. Its electric signal spreads a little inside the ear, an audiologist tunes how loud each slice feels, and every ear and every brain is different.

The cleaned-up clips show what the implant would be given if this model worked in front of it. They are an experiment on the sound itself, not a promise that Graham, or anyone, would understand more. Only testing with real listeners could show that, and it has not been done yet.

Part 3 · The hardest crowd

−5 dB is where it stops.

When the crowd is louder than the voice, the program stops helping enough.

What the numbers mean

Engineers measure how clean a sound is in decibels (dB). It is a squeezed scale: every 3 dB of improvement cuts the leftover noise, compared with the voice, in half.

  • In everyday crowd noise, the model cuts the leftover noise to less than half (+3.51 dB).
  • When the crowd is as loud as the voice (0 dB), it cuts it to about a third (+4.65 dB).
  • When the crowd is louder than the voice (−5 dB, about three times the voice's energy), it only cuts it by about a quarter (+1.19 dB). Afterwards the crowd is still more than twice as strong as the voice. Our goal there was +1.5 dB, which would have cut it by about 30%.

Is +1.5 just a made-up number?

Partly, and on purpose. We set it ourselves before running any tests, so we could not move the goalposts after seeing results. It is half of our overall +3 dB goal, and +3 dB is in the range that lab noise-reduction systems have reached in published studies with cochlear-implant listeners (those studies used a different measure, so it's a rough guide). Missing by 0.3 dB is not a disaster. It means this model is not yet strong enough in the hardest rooms to earn a listening test.

Why did we miss?

At −5 dB, the crowd is made of other voices that sound a lot like the one you want. The model has one microphone, cannot hear the future (it has to answer within 6 thousandths of a second), and is tiny on purpose so it could one day run on a hearing device. A "cheating" version that hears the future and already knows the answer gets about +12 dB here, so there is room to improve. More training and two different scoring changes did not close the gap. Next, we train on far more hard examples, and after that we give the model two ears so it can use direction as a clue.

The short version

The question

Can a tiny program that never peeks ahead clean up crowd noise for cochlear-implant users well enough to earn a real listening test? We judge it on computer tests, with the passing scores fixed before we start.

What we found

Our best version so far, number 19, passes 13 of 14 checks. In everyday crowd noise it cuts the leftover noise to less than half. In the hardest crowds, where the crowd is louder than the voice, it falls just short of the score we set. Four later versions tried to fix that. Three did not help, and one is being tested right now.

What it does not mean

We have not compared it with the Nucleus 6 processors Graham already wears, tested it on any implant, or played it to any listener. Everything here is from computer tests. Our own verdict is: not good enough yet, keep working. No version has been approved for anything.

Why 14 checks?

Each check answers a question a parent or an audiologist would ask about a noise-reduction program.

  • Does it help in a noisy crowd? (3 checks: how clean the voice is, an estimate of how understandable it is, and what the implant's electrodes would actually receive)
  • Does it still help in the hardest crowds, when the crowd is as loud as the voice or louder? (2 checks)
  • Does it ever make speech harder to understand there? (1 check)
  • Does it work every time we train it, not just once? (1 check: we train it three separate times from scratch, so one lucky run cannot fool us)
  • Is it better than the simple noise filter that already exists? (1 check)
  • Does it cope with other kinds of noise, like a steady hum or a hiss it has never heard before? (2 checks)
  • Does it leave speech alone when it is already quiet? (2 checks)
  • Does it keep the clues that tell you which side a voice is coming from? (2 checks)

That is 14. We wrote them all down, with the score each one has to reach, before running the tests. That way we could not choose the flattering numbers afterwards. The model passes 13. The one it misses is the hardest crowd, where the crowd is louder than the voice.

How we improve it, one change at a time

Each new version changes exactly one thing. We write down what we expect before we train it, train it three separate times, and let a script, not a person, decide whether it passed. Three tries at the hardest crowd, none of them worked

Version 21: training longer did not help

We tried two ways of training for twice as long, each three separate times. To count as a real improvement, the hardest crowd had to get at least 0.3 dB cleaner on both of our two test sets. Everyday crowds did get better. The hardest crowd did not, and on one test set it got slightly worse.

      What could this mean for Graham?

      Nothing on this page has been tried with Graham's implants, or with any listener. His Nucleus 6 processors are what he uses, and we have never compared our program with them. What follows is what the computer tests say, and what it could look like if every step below were passed.

      1 · Hearing a voice in a noisy room

      A tiny program would sit between the microphone and the implant. Every 6 thousandths of a second, it guesses which part of the sound is the voice and turns the rest down, using only sound that has already happened. It is small enough to imagine on a phone or inside a processor someday, though we have not timed it on one yet.

      What the computer tests say. In everyday crowd noise it cuts the leftover noise to less than half. When the crowd is as loud as the voice, to about a third. The pulses an implant would send also look more like the clean voice. Speech that is already quiet is left alone. In the hardest case, when the crowd is louder than the voice, it only cuts the noise by about a quarter, short of our goal. That is the wall.

      What it could look like. A birthday party, a cafeteria, a family dinner: the chatter turned down a little, the voice he is facing coming through clearer, quiet speech untouched. It could one day run on a phone or a clip-on gadget that sends sound to his processors, or inside a future processor.       2 · Knowing which side a voice comes from

      With two implants, each side cleans up sound mostly on its own. Your brain works out where a sound comes from partly from tiny differences between your ears: which ear hears it louder, and which hears it first. People with implants rely mostly on the louder-ear clue. If each side turns sound down by a different amount, that clue blurs, and a voice on his left might seem to come from the middle.

      How we keep ourselves honest

      We write the plan down first

      Before we train a new version we write down the one thing we are changing, how long we will train it, and the score it has to reach. Version 24 is drafted but does not count until version 23 has its result and its review.

      We train it three times

      Every version is trained three separate times from scratch and judged on the average, so one lucky run cannot fool us. A single good run only earns the right to be repeated.

      We judge the finished version only

      We save and report the program at several points during training, but the verdict always uses the final one. No picking the best moment after the fact.

      We replay interrupted runs exactly

      One training run was cut off halfway when a computer session ended. We restarted it from the beginning and checked that it reproduced the original, number for number, before its result counted.

      Someone else checks the sums

      A second AI, Codex Astra, made by a different company, recomputes every result from the raw data between versions. Its reviews are published word for word, including the ones that made us fix things before launch.

      Failures stay on the record

      Versions 16, 20, 21 and 22 did not help, and they are on the tree with their numbers. Not helping once does not prove an idea can never work, and the record says so.

      What this does not show

      • We never tested the Nucleus 6's own noise cleanup. Nothing here says our program is better than what Graham's processors already do.
      • We are not promising it helps anyone hear better. Everything is from computer tests. Even a hearing person listening to a simulation cannot tell you what an implant user would hear.
      • No version has earned a real listening test yet. That needs all 14 checks passed, a repeat on brand-new voices, and then an audiologist in charge.
      • Two of our own checks are still blank: how natural quiet speech sounds, and whether the program runs fast enough on an ordinary processor.
      • On hissing noise it had never heard, one score got slightly worse in all three training runs, even though the main hiss check passed.
      • The which-side checks come from one training run in four pretend rooms, and in the hardest room the louder-ear clue got worse. Nothing here shows better direction hearing.
      • Our earlier versions were sloppier. Version 17 changed several things at once, so we cannot say which one helped.

      How the tests work

      We take clean recordings of people reading aloud and mix in noise: a crowd of 8 to 12 other talkers, one other talker, a steady hum, a hiss, or nothing at all. We score each mix before and after the program cleans it, using scores that stand in for how clear and understandable the voice is, and one that pretends to be an implant. A separate test checks that the which-side clues survive.

      For researchers: methods, sources and downloads

      The bench mixes clean read speech (LibriSpeech, speaker-disjoint train and test sets) with noise: a crowd of 8 to 12 talkers drawn from six held-out interferer identities, a single competing talker, speech-shaped noise, held-out white noise, and quiet. Each mixture is scored before and after the model under three intelligibility proxies: waveform STOI, vocoded ESTOI and a simulated 8-of-22 ACE electrodogram correlation, plus SI-SDR. A spatial screen checks that direction cues (ILD, ITD) are not damaged on simulated scenes.

      Technical details

      • Model: causal Conv-TasNet-style enhancer, 602,510 parameters, past context 1.02 s, no look-ahead; 16 kHz.
      • Training (Gen 19): 24k steps, batch 8 × 2 s, 10% quiet examples, Adam, cosine 1e-3 → 1e-4, 3 seeds (20261117/18/19), 251-speaker train-clean-100 pool.
      • Bench v2: n = 6 crops per cell; SNRs −5/0/5/10 dB; systems: unprocessed, classical causal gate, non-causal oracle, candidate.
      • Scorecard: 14 required rows plus one diagnostic; verdicts PASS / FAIL / INCOMPLETE from kpi_scorecard.py; +3.0 dB crowd requirement is about a third of the oracle headroom.
      • Screens (Gen 21–24): candidate minus same-seed Gen 19 at −5 dB must be ≥ +0.30 dB on both the fixed VAL panel and the bench, with 0 dB, quiet and other-row safeguards.
      • Tests: 17 bench/spatial tests, 42 trainer/judge tests across Gen 20–24.

      Limitations of the bench itself

      • Synthetic mixtures of read speech; six interferer identities in the crowd pool.
      • Spatial scenes use an assumed head model; one seed.
      • The vocoder listening-pack builder does not yet enforce the current scorecard, checkpoint identity or fresh-material clearance.
