Inside the implant · what happens to sound

How a cochlear implant hears.

A hearing ear has thousands of tiny hair cells, each one listening for its own little sliver of pitch. A cochlear implant takes the place of all of them with one thin wire that has just 22 tiny electrodes along it, like trading thousands of piano keys for 22. Everything the microphone picks up, a voice, a crowd, a song, has to squeeze through those 22 spots.

  1. 22 slices of pitch

    The implant splits every sound into 22 slices of pitch, like 22 piano keys running from low to high. Each slice has its own tiny contact on the wire inside the ear. Low sounds go deep into the ear's spiral and high sounds stay near the opening, which is the same way a hearing ear sorts sound.

  2. Only about 8 at once

    Many times every second, the implant checks which slices are loudest, and only those get to play. Implants like Graham's commonly play about 8 of the 22 at a time, and an audiologist sets the exact number for each person. Think of a piano where only 8 keys can sound at once: the tune comes through, but a lot of it goes missing.

  3. What gets lost

    Inside each slice, all the fine detail is blended into one thing: how loud that slice is. That detail is what lets you hear a voice rise and fall, the warmth or scratchiness that makes someone sound like themselves, and the notes in a song. The rhythm of talking and the shape of words still come through surprisingly well. In a crowd, other voices compete for the same 8 keys, and the loudest ones are not always the voice you want.

Listen to what the squeeze does to a voice.

Researchers have a way to give people with typical hearing a rough idea of an implant. They split a recording into the same 22 slices, keep only the 8 loudest at each moment, and rebuild each slice out of soft hissing noise, so the fine detail disappears much as it does in an implant. These clips were made that way. They are about as close as someone with typical hearing can get to what Graham might hear, and they are still only a rough sketch. Every person with an implant hears differently, and a brain that grows up with one learns to make sense of it in ways no recording can show.

Each clip is the same sentence at the same loudness, about 8 seconds long. Only one plays at a time.

How these clips were made

The voices come from LibriSpeech, a free collection of volunteers reading books aloud. Nothing from Graham or his family is used here. The reader in these clips, and the people in the crowd around her, were never used to teach the model, so it is hearing all of them for the first time. The crowd is ten snippets of other readers talking at once, set to be as loud as her or a little louder.

The squeeze into 8 of 22 slices is the same one this research uses to score the model, and the moving picture above is drawn from those same numbers. A real implant is more complicated. Its electric signal spreads a little inside the ear, an audiologist tunes how loud each slice feels, and every ear and every brain is different.

The cleaned-up clips show what the implant would be given if this model worked in front of it. They are an experiment on the sound itself, not a promise that Graham, or anyone, would understand more. Only testing with real listeners could show that, and it has not been done yet.

Clips are 16 kHz mono mp3, loudness-matched to −20.5 LUFS, peaks at or below −1.3 dBFS, no clipped samples.
